1. Serving metrics

Serving has two parts: retrieval (finding the candidate ads) and ranking (ordering them).

Retrieval

MetricDefinitionMeasured on
Precision@8Relevant ads in the first 8 shown ÷ ads shown (up to 8)Queries where FCC showed ≥1 ad
Recall@8Relevant ads in the first 8 shown ÷ the smaller of (relevant ads, 8)Queries with ≥1 relevant ad
Correct no-ad rateShare of queries where FCC correctly showed nothingQueries with no relevant ad
Fill rate@8Ad slots filled in the first 8 ÷ 8All queries
  • Relevant = the LLM judged the ad right for the search; any product not labelled counts as not relevant. How relevance is graded, and the retailer relevance setting (future scope), are in section 6.
  • Each query counts equally. Search volume mix differs by client and changes over time, so it is not used as a weight. Head, Torso and Tail are reported separately instead.
  • A query with no relevant ad that still shows ads scores 0% Precision@8. That is how irrelevant serving is penalised.
  • Fill rate@8 is the counterweight to precision: showing nothing on hard queries keeps precision high but leaves slots empty.

Ranking

MetricDefinitionMeasured on
NDCG@8How close the order of the first 8 ads is to the ideal order (most relevant first), using the grades in section 6Queries where FCC showed ≥1 ad
MRRAverage of 1 ÷ the position of the first relevant ad (1 if it is in slot 1, ½ in slot 2, …; 0 if none)Queries where FCC showed ≥1 ad
Retrieval is the burning issue: every issue found so far is relevant ads not shown, or irrelevant ads shown.
So this exercise focuses on retrieval first. Ranking metrics are monitored from now on.

Where the gap comes from

The gap in sections 1–3 traces back to two sub-systems, and each needs its own way of being measured:
  • Category classifier: which categories a query is sent to. Measured in sections 4–7.
  • Taxonomy (category tree): how categories and products are organised. Measured in section 8.
Note: these are the known issues, and they are significantly critical. We fix these first, then move on to other enhancements.

4. Category classifier

Where it sits

Query clean-up & spell check→Brand detection→Category classifier→Product retrieval (ad products only)→Ranking→Top 8 shown
  • It scores 29 of Nahdi's 34 top-level categories for the query, using every product in Nahdi's catalog (~80k), not just the ad products. It never scores Home Care, Shopping Bags, Vitamins & Supplements, Medical Clothing or Medicines & Treatment.
  • It keeps the categories scoring ≥ 0.35 (at most 3). Retrieval then looks for ad products only inside those categories. A relevant ad outside them can never be shown.
PathWhat retrieval searchesQueries
Kept ≥1 categoryAd products in the kept categories7,447
Ran, kept no categoryAd products in all categories3,186
Skipped (brand-only query)Ad products in all categories1,678
This is FCC's output. For benchmarking, the approach is below (section 5).

Objective

Keep the categories the query is about, and only those.

What the earlier approach got wrong

  • It never asked which categories a query is about. A category counted as right only if one of the LLM's relevant ads was filed under it.
  • So the right answer depended on the ad pool and on how the catalog files products, not on the query:
    • If the right category had no ad products, the classifier was marked wrong by default. In 530 of the 896 queries where the classifier and the LLM shared no category, the classifier's category holds no ad products at all.
    • A relevant ad filed under an unexpected category made that category "right". "مرطب جسم" (body moisturiser) is about Bath & Body, but NIVEA Soft cream filed under Skin Care made Skin Care right, and a classifier that kept Bath & Body was penalised.
  • Catalog and ad-pool gaps were blamed on the classifier, and there was no way to see them separately.

5. Measuring the category classifier

The right categories come from the query alone. Where the relevant ads are filed is measured separately (section 7).

Metrics

MetricQuestion it answersDefinition
Category recall (primary)Did the classifier keep every category the query is about?Right categories kept ÷ all right categories
Category precision (secondary)Did it keep only those categories?Right categories kept ÷ all categories kept
No-decision rateHow often did the classifier run but keep nothing?Queries where it ran and kept no category ÷ queries where it ran
  • Recall comes first: a right category that is dropped loses its ads for good. Precision stops the classifier from scoring well by keeping everything.
  • Measured on all queries, each counting equally. The right categories don't depend on whether a relevant ad exists.

How they are counted

DecisionRule
CountingEach right category counts equally. Weighting by the number of relevant ads would give a right category with no ads zero weight, which brings back the ad-pool dependence described in section 4.
No category keptKept out of category recall and precision, and reported as the no-decision rate. Scoring it as "all categories kept" would give 100% recall for not deciding; leaving it out silently would hide it.
Brand-only queriesKept out of all classifier metrics and reported as a count. The classifier is skipped by design, so there is no decision to judge.
The two all-category paths look the same in today's output and are told apart from the brand fields. FCC should log which path each query took (kept / no decision / skipped), so they are explicit rather than inferred.

How right categories are labelled

One method for every retailer, with no retailer-specific rules: the LLM learns each category from the retailer's own category tree. Products are not used, because a retailer's tree can be crooked: Nahdi's has the same node under several top-level categories (e.g. Deodorant under Men Care and Bath & Body) and products filed on non-leaf nodes, so the products in a category don't reliably show what it means.
StepWhat happensInput
1. Describe each categoryFor each of the 34 top-level categories, the LLM reads its name and the names of all its sub-categories, and writes a description in the fields defined in section 6. Reviewed once, and again whenever the tree changes.Category tree (Nahdi category tree API)
2. Grade all 34 categories for every queryGrades as defined in section 6.The queries in lists A and B
  • Category level: top level (L1) for now. Later L2 and L3, labelled level by level: L1 first, then only the children of the right L1 categories.

Checks before the labels are trusted

  • Consistency: a sample is graded twice; the share of identical grades is reported.
  • Manual check: a person reviews grades for top queries.

6. Labelling definitions Locked

The single reference for what each label means. Every labelling run, for every retailer, follows these definitions. Changes are made here first.

General rules

  • Grade the query exactly as the user typed it, including misspellings and Arabic. FCC's clean-up and spell check are part of what is being judged.
  • Grade each category, and each product, on its own. There is no limit on how many get a grade, and all can be irrelevant.
  • The grader never sees FCC's output or which products are ads. For categories, it never sees products either.
  • When torn between two grades, choose the lower one.

Category description fields

FieldMeaningHow it is used in grading
CoversWhat the category is about, from its name and its main sub-categoriesDecides whether the category can be relevant
Also containsSub-categories that sit here in the tree but fall outside what the name implies (e.g. coffee makers under Sport Supplements)Can make the category somewhat relevant at most
Overlaps between categories (e.g. Deodorant under Men Care and Bath & Body) are not used in grading: every query is graded against all 34 categories anyway. They are reported to the catalog owner as duplicate nodes (section 8).

Query → category grades

GradeDefinition
RelevantThe category's Covers includes what the user is looking for, and nothing in the query rules it out. If several categories' Covers include it, all of them are relevant; a category is not marked down for being less specific.
Somewhat relevantThe query matches only the category's Also contains; or the category covers a less likely meaning of an ambiguous query; or it covers a close substitute a shopper might accept instead.
IrrelevantNone of the above.
Example: "مزيل عرق رجالي" (men's deodorant). Men Care: relevant (covers men's deodorant). Bath & Body: relevant (covers deodorant, not limited to women). Fragrances: somewhat relevant (close substitute). Kids Care: irrelevant (covers kids deodorant; "men's" rules it out).

Query → product grades

GradeDefinition
RelevantThe product is the type of product searched for and fits every attribute the query states: brand, product line, variant, audience (men, women, kids, baby), form, size, or concern (e.g. dandruff, sensitive skin).
Somewhat relevantThe right type of product for the same need, but it misses one stated attribute, so a shopper could reasonably accept it instead.
IrrelevantA different type of product or need, or it misses more than one stated attribute. Products used together with what was searched (e.g. conditioner for a shampoo search) are irrelevant.
Example: "head and shoulders shampoo". Any Head & Shoulders shampoo: relevant. Another brand's anti-dandruff shampoo: somewhat relevant (misses brand). Johnson's baby shampoo: irrelevant (misses brand, concern and audience). Head & Shoulders conditioner: irrelevant (different product type).

Query types

Query typeRule
Brand only (e.g. "nivea")Products: any product of that brand is relevant; other brands are irrelevant. Categories: those covering what the brand mainly sells are relevant.
Product type only (e.g. "shampoo")Any product of that type is relevant, whatever the brand.
Ambiguous (e.g. "غسول" = face, body or mouth wash)Grade against the most likely meaning; the other plausible meanings are somewhat relevant.

Relevance setting Future scope

Many cases are neither clearly right nor clearly wrong, so the retailer will decide how strict FCC should be. Counting only relevant ads keeps precision high but leaves more slots empty; also counting somewhat relevant ads fills more slots with less clear-cut ads.
SettingCounts as right, for products and categories alike
StrictRelevant only
BroadRelevant + somewhat relevant
  • Labels are graded once, so every metric can be reported under both settings from the same labels. Until a retailer setting exists, both are reported.
  • To be checked against clicks: if somewhat relevant ads earn clicks close to relevant ones, the broad setting is worth it.

7. Catalog fit

Compares where the relevant ads are filed with the categories the query is about. It shows ads that no classifier could reach, because they are filed under a category the query isn't about.
MetricDefinitionMeasured on
Catalog fitRelevant ads filed under a right category ÷ all relevant adsQueries with ≥1 relevant ad
  • Counted per ad, because filing is decided product by product: each misfiled ad is one filing error.
  • Example: for "مرطب جسم" (body moisturiser), the right category is Bath & Body. Of 10 relevant ads, 8 are filed under Bath & Body and 2 under Skin Care, so catalog fit = 80%. The 2 Skin Care ads are lost whatever the classifier does.
  • The fix is in the catalog: re-file the product, or file it under more than one category.

How it connects to the classifier

Relevant ad is…Classifier kept its categoryClassifier didn't keep it
Filed under a right categoryPasses to retrievalLost: classifier error
Filed under a wrong categoryPasses by luckLost: catalog error
Every relevant ad the classifier filters out is either a classifier error or a catalog error, and each shows up in its own metric.
