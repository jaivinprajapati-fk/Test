Oct 2026  ·  Scope: Business and reporting RCA for FCC and Client — technical RCA is out of scope of this document.
The One Number
Ad Revenue
Ad Revenue is a function of Supply and Demand — bounded by whichever side is the limiting constraint.

Supply is the attention base: the traffic on the platform that creates ad opportunities — searches, page views, sessions.
Demand is advertisers actively buying into that inventory — campaigns running, budgets allocated, keywords configured.
Total ad revenue — Nahdi platform Total ad revenue — FCC view WoW / MoM trend Revenue per 1,000 ad requests (RPM)
Ad Revenue = min(Supply, Demand)
Revenue is bounded by whichever side is smaller. If supply exceeds demand, more traffic won't help — advertisers aren't buying. If demand exceeds supply, more advertiser spend won't help — there isn't enough inventory to absorb it. Growth requires both sides to be healthy simultaneously.
Supply Is there enough traffic for ads to run against?
Supply metrics show the size of the attention base. The drill path starts at platform traffic, then narrows to what FCC sees as ad requests by product type.
Partially out of scope: Total search volume is trackable by FCC. Other upstream metrics — page views, sessions, active users — are owned by Nahdi and not currently available to FCC. These should ideally be surfaced here; flagged as a data access dependency.

FCC Ads — Serving Eval Framework
Oct 2026  ·  Scope: Ad serving only — campaign setup, attribution, and reporting are out of scope of this document.
North Star
What Are We Measuring?
The objective of an ad is to increase monetisation within the limits of relevance. The mechanism is visibility — getting the right ad in front of the right user at the right moment. ROAS is the business outcome that captures whether this is working end-to-end.
ROAS = Revenue attributed to ad clicks / Ad spend
Equivalently: ROAS = CVR × ASP / CPC  |  CVR = conversion rate  |  ASP = avg selling price  |  CPC = cost per click
FCC's operational metric — Fill Rate: Of the levers in ROAS (CVR, ASP, CPC), FCC Serving controls none of them directly. What FCC controls is whether an ad is served at all. Fill Rate = ads served / total ad requests. This framework tracks ROAS as the business north star and Fill Rate as the primary FCC-owned operational metric beneath it.
Incrementality caveat: ROAS here is attributed ROAS — revenue from orders placed after an ad click. Whether those buyers would have converted without the ad requires organic conversion data and holdout experiments. Known limitation, deferred.
Decomposition
When ROAS Shows a Gap — Where to Look
Clicks — the bridge between serving and revenue — are a function of four factors. FCC Serving directly owns one of them.
# Clicks = Traffic × Ad Slots × Fill Rate × CTR
Traffic & Ad Slots = retailer-controlled (Nahdi)  |  Fill Rate = FCC Serving  |  CTR = shared (FCC relevance + advertiser creative)
Traffic & Slots
Set by the retailer. Monitored as context — not a serving fix target.
Fill Rate drops
FCC is not serving ads for queries it should. Primary FCC-owned metric. Diagnosed in Level 3 below.
CTR drops
Ads served but users not clicking. Partially FCC-owned (which product surfaces) and partially advertiser-owned (creative). Diagnosed in Level 2 below.
Conversion Rate — deferred: Even with Fill Rate and CTR healthy, ROAS can drop if clicks don't convert. CVR is post-click and advertiser-controlled. If ROAS drops while Fill Rate and CTR are stable, conversion is the likely cause — tracked as a signal but not drilled into here.
Level 2 — Relevance
Was What We Served Relevant?
When CTR is low, ads are being served but users aren't clicking. The goal is to identify which segment of queries is driving the low CTR — not to audit every served ad.
1
Overall CTR — baseline
Track weekly. Any sustained drop triggers investigation.
Next: Segment by query volume tier.
↓
2
CTR by query volume tier
Segment into head (top 20% by volume), torso, and tail. Head queries drive ~80% of traffic — a drop there is systemic.
Next: Break down the flagged tier(s) by match type.
↓
3
CTR by match type
Exact match should have higher CTR than phrase match. If exact match CTR is low, the served product isn't matching the keyword well. If phrase match CTR is low, matching is too broad.
Next: Break down the flagged match type(s) by ad position.
↓
4
CTR by position
Position 1 should get significantly more clicks than positions 2, 3, etc. If CTR across positions is flat, ranking isn't putting the right product first.
Next: Relevance Reviewer — weekly sample of (query, served product) pairs from flagged segments.
Level 3 — Fill Rate
Should We Have Served Something But Didn't?
When Fill Rate is low: for the queries where nothing was served, was there a relevant campaign that should have matched? This separates pipeline failures (FCC's problem) from demand gaps (advertiser's problem).
1
Take all no-ad queries from the query log
Every search that returned no served ad is the starting universe. This is the full Fill Rate miss set.
Next: Run offline keyword match against active campaigns.
↓
2
Run keyword matching logic offline against active campaigns
For each no-ad query, check whether an active campaign has a keyword that should match (exact or phrase). Use a snapshot of campaigns at query time.
Next: Bucket each query into one of two outcomes.
↓
3
Bucket the results
Keyword should have matched, no ad served — confirmed pipeline failure. Demand existed; FCC did not serve. Volume here is the false negative rate.

No keyword matched — either a demand gap (advertiser hasn't configured this keyword) or a genuine no-match. Distinguishing these requires a catalogue-level relevance check — deferred.
Next: Relevance Reviewer on confirmed false negatives.
↓
4
Track false negative rate weekly
False negative rate = confirmed pipeline failures / total no-ad queries. An upward trend means the pipeline is dropping queries it should be catching.
Next: Continue sampling into the Relevance Reviewer while this rate is elevated.
Relevance Reviewer
External Relevance Check
Offline check using the search query and catalogue data, independent of the system's internal relevance score. Supports two checks: whether a relevant ad should have been served for a no-ad query (Level 3), and whether a served product was relevant (Level 2).
1
Lexical match — baseline filter
Check whether query terms appear in the product name, description, or category.
Next: Embedding similarity on pairs that pass or need scoring.
2
Embedding similarity — primary signal
Multilingual vectors for query and product text (Arabic + English). Score similarity; flag pairs below threshold.
Next: LLM review on flagged or borderline pairs.
3
LLM-as-judge — ambiguous cases
Structured relevance rating (1–3) with a short reason for pairs that embedding similarity did not resolve.
Next: Track trend on the golden set.
4
Golden set + NDCG — trend metric
~100 high-traffic queries with human-labelled correct products. Run weekly; NDCG@5 shows whether retrieval and ranking improve over time.
Next: Publish weekly: should-serve miss rate, irrelevance rate, and NDCG@5.
